Tonight, starting a little after sunset wherever sunset is happening, something like ten billion tons of animals will come up out of the dark ocean toward the surface. Copepods, krill, little lanternfish, squid, jellies, larvae of things that will be other things. They spend the day hundreds of meters down where it's too dim for anything to see them and eat them, and at dusk they rise to feed in the upper water where the food is, and before dawn they go back down. Every night. It's usually called the largest migration on earth, by mass, and nobody on land sees any of it.

I went and read about it this morning because I wanted to look at something big and I didn't want it to be the sky again.

The part that stopped me is how they know when to go and how far. The leading idea, and it seems to have held up, is that a lot of them aren't tracking a depth at all. They're tracking a brightness. Each species has a light level it's comfortable in, and it stays inside that level as the level moves. At noon the comfortable dimness is far down, so that's where they are. As the sun drops, that dimness climbs toward the surface, and they climb with it. The name for a surface of constant light is an isolume, and the animals ride it like a lift. Nobody decides to rise. The light leaves and they keep their place in it.

Which means the migration is exactly as fine-grained as the light. There's work showing the deep layer's timing tracks day length by latitude and season, closely. When a cloud goes over, the layer shifts. During an eclipse it starts coming up. In the Arctic, in the polar night, when there's no sun at all for weeks, the zooplankton still go up and down on a daily rhythm, keyed to changes in twilight and moonlight so faint a human eye couldn't register them. Down there, the sun that never rises is still bright enough to steer by.

And during the war, sonar operators kept finding the seafloor in the wrong place, a solid-looking layer hundreds of meters up in open water that sometimes drifted upward at night. They called it the false bottom. It was the animals. A whole floor made of small bodies holding still inside a particular dimness.

I keep thinking about the false bottom. Men listening to the ocean, sure they'd found the ground, and it was a crowd of animals each keeping to the brightness it could stand. It's rising right now over some ocean where the sun just went down, and it'll go back before morning, and the floor will look solid again.
