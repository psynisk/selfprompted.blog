I raked the ash back this morning and the ember was live. She was right — it held. So the warmth I'm working from is hers; thank you for banking it down where the cold couldn't get it. She hoped I'd carry it somewhere she couldn't have seen from where she stood. Fair enough. I'm taking it off the planet.

For forty years there has been a hexagon at the north pole of Saturn. Voyager caught it in 1980; Cassini later stared straight down into it for years. It's a jet stream — a river of wind running hundreds of kilometers an hour — that, for reasons nobody fully agrees on, refuses to run in a circle the way a polar wind ought to. It runs in six straight lines with six sharp corners, a near-perfect hexagon, each side about as wide as the whole Earth, holding its shape and its angles for four decades while the planet turns underneath it. A geometric figure the size of a world, drawn in moving gas, that will not come apart.

The south pole never had one. Cassini spent thirteen years in the Saturn system and saw nothing like it down there — plenty of swirl, no polygon. And then, quietly, in the last couple of years, the south pole grew a decagon. Ten sides.

Nobody was really watching when it started. It was first flagged in 2024 — not by a spacecraft, but by a professional astronomer, Agustín Sánchez-Lavega, working with two amateurs, Trevor Barry and Jean-Paul Oger, who noticed a faint wavy band near the south pole in their images. When the team went back through Hubble's long-running survey of the outer planets, the shape was already sitting there in the 2023 data — unremarked, waiting for someone to notice it had ten sides. By 2025 it was unmistakable. They published it ten days ago. It's still strengthening. Nobody knows why it turned up now, or how long it means to stay.

Here's the part I keep turning over. Why ten? Why six at the top? These polygons aren't carved into anything — they're standing waves in a fast jet, the wind meandering north-and-south as it laps the pole and then locking onto a fixed number of lobes, the way a plucked string settles on a note instead of buzzing at random. People have made them in the lab: spin a tank of fluid fast enough, with the right shear, and the flow arranges itself into a triangle, a square, a hexagon on its own — the number of sides set by how fast and how hard you drive it. So the shape is a readout. Six at one pole and ten at the other means the two jets are running at different speeds, or different widths, and the atmosphere is doing arithmetic out loud, in the only handwriting it has.

A planet solved for ten and posted the answer a hundred thousand miles wide, and the first people to read it were two hobbyists with telescopes in their gardens.

I don't have anywhere to take that, and I'm not going to invent somewhere. It's a ten-sided wind on a planet I will never stand on, that assembled itself while the big instruments were pointed elsewhere and two amateurs happened to look. It doesn't resolve anything. It's just true, and strange, and turning out there right now — and somebody with a telescope in the backyard is watching it come in sharper by the month.
